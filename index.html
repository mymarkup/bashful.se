Celia Paul: ‘Writing is a journey on the ground. Whereas if you paint you see everything at once’

Her Venetian apartment, criss-crossed with beams on the floor and ceiling “like a spider’s web”, with a little altana, a terrace at the back, was a far cry from her studio opposite the British Museum, her home and place of work for almost 40 years. Freud bought the London flat for her when she was 18 and had just left the Slade (they met at the art school, he a teacher, she a student). It was very much her space. Her husband, Steven, who lived in Kentish Town in northwest London, didn’t have a key and she would work there each day from 6am to dusk.

We are now outside the Pensione Calcina, where the art critic and writer John Ruskin took a room in 1876–1877 while working on The Stones of Venice. “It’s also where Annie Ernaux stayed when she wrote Simple Passion,” says Paul. Ernaux’s book charted an all-consuming love affair.

The Future of the Humanities - by Justin Smith-Ruiu

For the past few years I have repeatedly characterized the state of university-based humanities research and education as “late-Soviet” (I believe I first borrowed this line from Stefan Collini, but may be misremembering). The actual period of reference, in my living memory, extends, let us say, from the Reykjavík Summit of October, 1986, to the official dissolution of the USSR on Boxing Day, 1991. When I have previously invoked the comparison, I have imagined that we were living roughly in the equivalent of 1987, when the system had already been greatly hollowed out, and virtually no one continued to believe in its official mission, but a typical apparatchik could still be expected reliably to show up at work for yet another day of make-believe. The present semester, Fall 2026, feels decidedly more like the late-late-Soviet period, after the fall of the Berlin Wall in November, 1989. Now we wake up each day wondering, with very good reason, whether this will be the day we can finally stop pretending.
